# Fixed layers in a four-box stack

Suppose a binary $5\times7\times53$ stack has exactly four monochromatic boxes and admits a nonidentity symmetry $h$. The [first-height argument](first_forced_height.md) shows that $h$ preserves colors, exchanges three disjoint column pairs, and acts on the rows by one or two transpositions. All layers are distinct and minimum. After a global color interchange we may assume two rows have total weight 186 and three have weight 185. Each pair in the first class has one doubled red slot. Each pair in the second class has one doubled blue slot. All remaining colored slots occur once. Write $f$ for the number of layers fixed by $h$.

**Proposition.** In both row actions, $f\in\{5,7\}$. In the single-transposition case the exchanged rows form the two-row class and $f=5$. The three blue doubles occur on different exchanged column pairs, each in two fixed layers. The red double is supplied by exactly one orbit of two nonfixed layers.

**Proof.** Fix an exchanged row pair $A$. In a fixed layer its rows agree at the unique fixed column. In each color their total number of agreeing columns is one or two. All their other agreements come in exchanged column pairs. Hence $A$ agrees at exactly one exchanged column pair, in the color opposite to its fixed-column bit. It occupies one of the six fixed colored slots on $A$.

The unique doubled slot on $A$ is invariant under $h$, so these six fixed slots have seven occurrences in the stack. An occupied fixed slot lies in a fixed layer unless it is doubled by a pair of nonfixed layers. The fixed layers therefore supply five or seven occurrences on $A$, one each. If two row pairs are exchanged, their two doubled slots have the same origin, both from nonfixed pairs when $f=5$ and both from fixed layers when $f=7$.

Now suppose that $A$ is the only exchanged row pair and write $T$ for the three fixed rows. At each exchanged column pair $X$, a fixed layer contributes one or three fixed rectangles on $T\times X$. This counts the equal-color pairs among three bits. Nonfixed layers contribute in pairs. Since $f$ is odd, the total on $T\times X$ is odd. There are six available colored slots there, so at least one is doubled. The three different choices of $X$ require three different doubled slots within $T$. Thus $T$ is the three-row class and its three edges use three different exchanged column pairs.

In a fixed layer, two rows of $T$ use one fixed slot if they agree in the fixed column and two if they disagree. Indeed, outside this column the agreement count in each color is even, while its total over the layer is one or two. If $f=7$, any two rows of $T$ must agree in the fixed column of every fixed layer, since their six fixed slots have only seven occurrences. The column is balanced, so the common $T$ bit is opposite the common $A$ bit. Among the seven fixed layers $A$ uses a red slot four times, giving four red fixed-column bits for each row of $T$. The nonfixed layers contribute an even total to the weight of each fixed row. In every fixed layer the three exchanged column pairs also contribute an even total to that row. Its weight would be even, contradicting 185.

It follows that $f=5$ and the red double on $A$ comes from a nonfixed layer pair. Let $u$ count the blue doubles supplied by nonfixed pairs. The fixed layers then use $28-2(1+u)=26-2u$ fixed slots with multiplicity. A fixed layer uses four fixed slots when the three $T$ bits in its fixed column are equal, and six otherwise. Hence exactly $3-u$ fixed layers have mixed $T$ bits in that column.

In the five fixed layers $A$ uses two red slots and three blue slots, so its fixed-column bit is red three times. Giving each row of $T$ the opposite bit in all five layers would supply it with two red bits. In a mixed layer, balance changes exactly one of those three bits. Each fixed row must have an odd number of red fixed-column bits, since its total weight is 185. At least three changes are needed. Therefore $u=0$, and each of the fixed rows changes once. This proves the proposition.

## An exact-cover reduction for a single row transposition

Use zero-based indices. Take the exchanged rows to be $0,1$ and the exchanged column pairs to be $01,23,45$. By relabeling, prescribe the red double on rows $01$ and columns $01$. Prescribe the three blue doubles on row and column pairs $(23,01)$, $(24,23)$, and $(34,45)$. Every supporting pattern allowed by the proposition has this form up to relabeling.

There are 24 fixed colored slots. The other 396 slots form 198 two-element orbits. The five fixed layers use 40 rectangles altogether. Of these 26 are fixed slots counted with multiplicity. The remaining 14 fill seven of the 198 two-element orbits.

A nonfixed layer pair that supplies the red double fills seven ordinary two-element orbits. Each of the other 23 nonfixed layer pairs fills eight ordinary two-element orbits. A four-box certificate with this row action is equivalent to a partition of the 198 ordinary orbits. Its parts are

- one seven-element set supplied by five admissible fixed layers,
- one seven-element set supplied by a nonfixed pair duplicating the prescribed red slot,
- 23 eight-element sets supplied by nonfixed pairs with no fixed or repeated slot.

Here admissibility for the five fixed layers means that they supply exactly the prescribed multiplicities at the fixed slots, use no ordinary slot twice, and meet the parity condition on the three fixed rows. Each set must have a witness of the specified kind. From such a partition, the witnesses give 53 layers, with every ordinary slot used once and exactly four doubles among the fixed slots.

Even before imposing the total row weights, a family of five fixed layers supplies either none or all three of the blue doubles. To see this, encode each fixed row $i$ by its word of five fixed-column bits, with weight $n_i$. Put $b_{ij}=1$ when its blue double is supplied by the fixed layers, and zero otherwise. The Hamming distance between words $i,j$ is $2b_{ij}$, since they supply $5+2b_{ij}$ fixed slots in total. In a fixed layer the red-minus-blue fixed-slot count for $ij$ is one minus the sum of their fixed-column bits. Counting the three red and $2+2b_{ij}$ blue fixed slots across layers therefore gives $n_i+n_j=4+2b_{ij}$.

A single doubled edge is impossible because the other two distances would be zero. If two edges are doubled, write $i$ for their common vertex. Its word has weight four, while the other two words are identical and have weight two. Each differs from the word at $i$ in two positions, so its two ones are also ones at $i$. The fixed-column triples thus include a uniform blue position and a mixed position with two blue bits and one red bit.

In a layer with a uniform blue triple, each pair of fixed rows agrees at exactly one exchanged column pair, in red. The three exchanged pairs each contain an agreeing row pair, so each has a red agreeing pair. In a mixed layer with two blue bits, the three row pairs agree on respectively one, two and two exchanged column pairs. An exchanged pair accounts for one or three such agreements, so some exchanged pair has all three bits equal. The rows with blue fixed-column bits agree there and must therefore be red. This supplies every red slot of the three fixed rows on that exchanged column pair. The uniform blue layer would repeat one of those red slots, which have capacity one. This excludes two doubled edges.

For the admissible families $b_{ij}=1$ on every edge and hence $n_i=3$ for all three fixed rows. Giving them the bit opposite to that of rows $0,1$ in every fixed column would give each two red bits. The one change per row proved above must therefore be from blue to red. The five fixed-column vectors, in row order, are exactly
$$
00111,\quad 00111,\quad 11100,\quad 11010,\quad 11001.
$$
At each of the three exchanged column pairs, the fixed layers supply seven rectangles on the three fixed rows. Exactly one of the five layers has a uniform triple there, since every layer contributes one or three rectangles. This is a mixed fixed-column layer, where the uniform triple is red. Thus the three mixed layers biject to the three exchanged column pairs and supply all red fixed slots of the three fixed rows.

The seven ordinary orbits supplied by the fixed layers draw on only **42 of the 198** possible ordinary orbits. Five are blue, using one row from each weight class and columns from two different exchanged pairs. The two red orbits also cross the row classes. One is on column pair $23$ and the other on $45$. There are 36 such blue orbits and three red choices on each of $23,45$.

Indeed, the agreeing columns on an invariant row pair form an invariant set in each color. Whenever this set has size two, it is an exchanged column pair. Thus every ordinary rectangle uses a row of $A$ and a row of $T$. If a layer has $a$ columns of weight three, its red rectangle count is $7+2a-10=2a-3$, by counting red row-pair agreements. For the five fixed layers $a$ is odd, as the fixed column has weight three. The red rectangle count is consequently three modulo four. A mixed fixed-column layer has three red fixed rectangles and only two ordinary rectangles, so both ordinary rectangles are blue. In its uniform red exchanged triple there can be no blue rectangle. On either of its other exchanged pairs the $T$ triple has two blue bits. A rectangle in blue crossing from $A$ to $T$ on the same exchanged pair would require both $A$ bits blue by invariance, contradicting balance. Its ordinary rectangle orbit therefore joins two different exchanged pairs.

In a uniform fixed-column layer, all three exchanged triples have two blue bits, and the red singleton runs through the three rows of $T$. There is one red fixed rectangle, so two of its four ordinary rectangles are red and two blue. A blue ordinary rectangle cannot lie within an exchanged column pair, since the two $A$ rows in this layer agree in blue only at the fixed column. In red, a given row of $T$ can agree with a row of $A$ only in the exchanged pair where that $T$ row is red. An ordinary red rectangle must therefore lie in this pair, where both $A$ rows are also red. The two uniform fixed-column layers use the two unmarked exchanged pairs for their red rectangle on $A$, proving the stated support restriction.

The [standard-library enumeration](check_four_box_involutions.py) finds 288 possible fixed minimum layers for the single row transposition, 144 using four fixed slots and 144 using six. For the normalized doubled slots it first allows each blue double to be supplied by either fixed or nonfixed layers. This gives 74,240 unordered fixed-layer families. Among them, 24,576 supply none of the blue doubles and 49,664 supply all three. Exactly the latter meet the row-weight parity condition, and they give 26,288 distinct seven-element ordinary-orbit supports. For the double row transposition it finds 480 possible fixed minimum layers, all using two fixed slots. These counts describe candidates for completion.

Only **1,681** of the 26,288 supports are inequivalent. The remaining relabelings independently reverse the three exchanged column pairs and may simultaneously swap rows 2 and 3 and column pairs $23$ and $45$. Swapping rows 0 and 1 acts on these families as the product of all three column reversals, since their composition is $h$. These 16 actions give 3,164 orbits on the 49,664 fixed-layer families and 1,681 on their supports.

## Two row transpositions

Write $A$ for the exchanged pair forming the two-row class and $B$ for the exchanged pair in the three-row class. A balanced fixed column has opposite bits on $A$ and $B$. Their unique fixed slots in that layer therefore have opposite colors. Let $X$ be the exchanged column pair carrying the red double on $A$, and $Y$ the pair carrying the blue double on $B$.

Exactly three fixed layers supply blue on $A$ and red on $B$. In these layers each of the three exchanged column pairs is used once on $A$ and once on $B$, so the assignment from the former to the latter is a permutation. The other $f-3$ fixed layers supply red on $A$ and blue on $B$. At $f=5$, they biject the two column pairs other than $X$ to the two other than $Y$. At $f=7$, they match the multiset consisting of all three column pairs with $X$ repeated to the analogous multiset with $Y$ repeated. These claims follow by counting the six available slots of each color across the two row pairs. The enumeration finds 16 choices of fixed layer for each choice of opposite colors with coincident column pairs, and 32 for each choice with distinct column pairs.

The other two blue doubles lie on the edges from $B$ to the fixed row. They form a two-element orbit, determined by the column pair on one of the two edges. There are 15 placements of the four doubles up to the relabelings preserving the involution and row classes. If $X=Y$, the remaining column pair has six types. It can be an exchanged pair (the marked pair or an unmarked pair), join two exchanged pairs (one marked or both unmarked), or join the fixed column to a marked or unmarked pair. If $X\ne Y$, the three exchanged pairs are distinguished. The remaining column pair can be one of these three pairs, join two of them, or join one of them to the fixed column. This gives nine types. Independent endpoint swaps in the exchanged pairs realize the equivalences within every type.

There are 204 ordinary two-element slot orbits for this involution. The fixed layers use $3f$ ordinary orbit incidences, and the nonfixed layers use the remaining $205-3f$. Exactly one ordinary orbit must be used twice.
